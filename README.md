# Can a Machine Tell Which Roman Workshop Made a Jar?

An analysis of 470 Roman olive oil amphorae (Dressel 20) from five workshops in Baetica
(present-day Andalusia, Spain), using machine learning to find out whether a jar's shape
reveals where it was made.

## Motivation

During the Roman Empire, millions of amphorae carried olive oil from southern Spain to the
rest of the empire. Archaeologists often find these jars far from where they were made, and
identifying the workshop that produced a jar helps reconstruct ancient trade routes.

This project asks whether simple shape measurements are enough for a model to identify a
jar's workshop, which measurements matter most, and whether neighboring workshops produced
more similar jars, which might suggest they shared or copied techniques.

## Questions

1. Which shape measurements best distinguish one workshop's jars from another's?
2. Did workshops located closer together produce more similar jars?
3. How accurately can a model identify which workshop made a jar?
4. If an archaeologist finds a new jar, which workshop most likely made it?

## Summary of Results

**1. The rim is the workshop's fingerprint.** Permutation importance shows that how far the
rim protrudes and how wide it is are the two most useful measurements. Delicias jars have a
much less protruding rim (median 14 mm vs 17–20 mm elsewhere), and Parlamento jars have a
narrower rim (32 mm vs 38–39 mm). Overall jar size and rim height contribute almost nothing.

**2. Neighboring workshops made more similar jars, but only very close neighbors.**
Distance and similarity are moderately related (Spearman correlation −0.39). The two closest
pairs, Belén–Malpica (11 km) and Delicias–Malpica (17 km), confuse about 23% and 15% of their
jars. All other pairs fall between 3% and 12%, regardless of distance. Delicias and Parlamento
are an exception: they are 126 km apart but still often confused, possibly because both
produced older Dressel B jars.

**3. The model identifies the right workshop about 3× better than chance.** A Random Forest
reaches **57% macro recall** in 5-fold cross-validation, compared with 49% for Logistic
Regression and 20% for a baseline that always guesses the largest workshop. Delicias is the
easiest workshop to identify and Villaseca the hardest.

**4. The model works best as a shortlist tool.** For a hypothetical jar with a narrow rim, the
model assigns 63% probability to Parlamento. For a jar with a short rim, it splits evenly
between Delicias and Parlamento (34% each). A what-if analysis shows that very short rims
(8–11 mm) point strongly to Delicias, while mid-length rims (17–24 mm) point to Belén.

## Limitations

- The dataset is small: 470 jars from 5 workshops, and only 10 workshop pairs for the
  distance analysis.
- Diameters appear to be rounded to the nearest 5–10 mm, which limits their usefulness.
- Jar type and chronology are linked to shape, so some differences between workshops may
  reflect *when* jars were made rather than *where*.
- The what-if analysis changes one measurement at a time, while real jars vary in all
  measurements together.
